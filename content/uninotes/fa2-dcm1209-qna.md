**1. Define partnership as per Section 4 of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932. Identify any three elements inherent in this definition.**

**Ans.**

**Definition and Elements of Partnership**

Section 4 of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 defines partnership as: “Partnership is the relation between persons who have agreed to share the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all.” Partnership is therefore a legal relationship created through an agreement between two or more persons to carry on a lawful business and share its profits. The persons are individually called partners, collectively called a firm, and the name under which the business is carried on is called the firm name. 

**A) Agreement Between Persons:**
Partnership arises from a contract between persons. The agreement may be express, either written or oral, or implied from the conduct of the parties. It is not created by status, inheritance, or merely by co-ownership of property. 

**B) Sharing of Profits:**
The partners must agree to share the profits of the business. Sharing of profits is an essential element that distinguishes partnership from relationships such as employment or agency. However, mere sharing of profits does not conclusively establish partnership. 

**C) Mutual Agency:**
The business must be carried on by all or any of the partners acting for all. Thus, every partner is both an agent and a principal of the firm. A partner can bind the firm and other partners by acts done in the ordinary course of business. Mutual agency is considered the real test of partnership. 

**Conclusion:**
Thus, agreement, sharing of profits, and mutual agency are three fundamental elements inherent in the statutory definition of partnership.

**2. What is a Partnership Deed? State any five items that are typically included in a Partnership Deed.**

**Ans.**

**Partnership Deed and Its Contents**

A Partnership Deed, also known as Articles of Partnership, is a written document containing the terms and conditions agreed upon by the partners. It governs the mutual rights, duties, and liabilities of the partners and provides a clear record of the arrangements governing the partnership. Although a written deed is not compulsory under the Indian Partnership Act, 1932, it is strongly advisable because it reduces disputes and enables partners to modify or exclude default provisions of the Act. 

**A) Names and Addresses of Partners:**
The deed contains the full names, addresses, and other identifying details of all the partners.

**B) Capital Contribution:**
It specifies the amount of capital to be contributed by each partner, the method of contribution, and whether capital is maintained on a fixed or fluctuating basis.

**C) Profit and Loss Sharing Ratio:**
The deed states the ratio in which the net profit or loss of the firm is to be shared among the partners. If it is not specified, profits are shared equally. 

**D) Interest on Capital and Drawings:**
It specifies whether interest is allowed on partners’ capital and whether interest is charged on amounts withdrawn by partners.

**E) Remuneration to Partners:**
The deed may provide for salary, commission, or other remuneration payable to active partners for their services. 

**Conclusion:**
Thus, a Partnership Deed clearly establishes the terms governing the partnership and helps ensure smooth management and fewer disputes among partners.

**4. What are the default provisions of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 regarding (a) profit-sharing ratio, (b) interest on capital, and (c) interest on a partner's loan to the firm?**

**Ans.**

**Default Provisions of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932**

In the absence of a Partnership Deed, or when the Partnership Deed is silent on a particular matter, the provisions of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 apply by default. These provisions regulate the distribution of profits and the treatment of interest relating to partners’ capital and loans. 

**A) Profit-Sharing Ratio:**
Under Section 13(b), profits and losses of the firm are shared equally among all partners, regardless of the amount of capital contributed by each partner. Therefore, unequal capital contributions do not affect the default profit-sharing ratio. 

**B) Interest on Capital:**
Under Section 13(c), no interest is payable to partners on their capital contributions when there is no agreement providing for such interest. Therefore, a partner cannot claim interest on capital merely because capital has been contributed to the firm. 

**C) Interest on Partner’s Loan to the Firm:**
Under Section 13(d), when a partner advances a loan to the firm beyond his agreed capital contribution, interest is payable on that loan at the rate of 6% per annum. Such a loan is treated separately from the partner’s capital contribution. 

**Conclusion:**
Thus, in the absence of a Partnership Deed, profits and losses are shared equally, no interest is allowed on capital, and interest at 6% per annum is payable on a partner’s loan to the firm.

**1. Discuss in detail the essential characteristics of a partnership under the Indian Partnership Act, 1932. Why is mutual agency considered the most critical test of partnership?**

**Ans.**

**Essential Characteristics of a Partnership under the Indian Partnership Act, 1932**

Partnership is a legal relationship between two or more persons who agree to share the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all. Section 4 of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 provides the statutory basis of partnership. For an arrangement to qualify as a partnership, it must possess certain essential characteristics. 

**A) Agreement Between Persons:**
Partnership arises from a contract between the persons concerned. The agreement may be express, either written or oral, or implied from their conduct. Partnership is not created by status, inheritance, or merely by co-ownership of property. A written Partnership Deed is preferable because it provides clarity and helps resolve disputes. 

**B) Two or More Persons:**
A minimum of two persons is necessary to form a partnership. The partners must be competent to contract, meaning they must be of the age of majority, of sound mind, and not disqualified by law. A minor cannot become a partner but may be admitted to the benefits of an existing partnership with the consent of all partners. 

**C) Lawful Business:**
The partnership must be formed to carry on a lawful business. If the object of the partnership is unlawful, the agreement is void and no valid partnership comes into existence. The term business includes every trade, occupation, and profession. 

**D) Sharing of Profits:**
The partners must agree to share the profits of the business. The profit-sharing ratio is generally specified in the Partnership Deed. If no ratio is specified, profits and losses are shared equally. However, mere sharing of profits does not conclusively establish partnership. 

**E) Mutual Agency:**
The business must be carried on by all or any of the partners acting for all. Therefore, every partner is both an agent and a principal of the firm. A partner can bind the firm and other partners by acts done in the ordinary course of business. 

**F) Mutual Agency as the Real Test:**
Mutual agency is considered the most critical characteristic because it distinguishes partnership from other forms of association. In a partnership, every partner is an implied agent of the firm and can bind it through acts within the scope of the firm's business. Thus, even where profits are shared, the absence of mutual agency means that the arrangement is not a partnership in the legal sense. 

**Conclusion:**
Thus, agreement, two or more persons, lawful business, profit sharing, and mutual agency are the essential characteristics of partnership. Among these, mutual agency is the real test because it establishes the legal relationship of partners as both agents and principals of the firm.

**2. Explain the various types of partners recognised in partnership law. How does the liability and role of each type differ?**

**Ans.**

**Types of Partners and Their Roles and Liabilities**

The Indian Partnership Act, 1932 recognises different types of partners based on their role, participation in management, financial interest, and liability towards third parties. The principal types include active, sleeping, nominal, partner in profits only, minor, partner by estoppel or holding out, incoming, outgoing, and secret partners. 

**A) Active or Working Partner:**
An active partner participates in the management and day-to-day operations of the business. He is fully liable to third parties for the acts of the firm. He may also receive salary or commission in addition to his share of profits. 

**B) Sleeping or Dormant Partner:**
A sleeping partner contributes capital and shares profits and losses but does not participate in management. Despite being inactive, he is equally liable to third parties as an active partner. He is generally not entitled to remuneration. 

**C) Nominal or Ostensible Partner:**
A nominal partner lends his name to the firm without contributing capital or sharing profits. He has no interest in the firm's assets or business but is liable to third parties who deal with the firm relying on his name. 

**D) Partner in Profits Only:**
This partner shares only in the profits and, by agreement, is not liable for losses. However, persons sharing profits may be treated as partners towards third parties and may become liable to outsiders. 

**E) Minor Partner:**
A minor cannot become a full partner because of lack of contractual capacity. With the consent of all partners, a minor may be admitted to the benefits of an existing partnership. He shares profits but is not personally liable for the firm's acts during minority. 

**F) Partner by Estoppel or Holding Out:**
A person who represents himself as a partner, or knowingly permits others to represent him as one, becomes liable to third parties who give credit to the firm based on that representation. He is not a real partner and has no rights in the firm. 

**G) Secret, Incoming and Outgoing Partners:**
A secret partner participates actively in management but is not publicly known as a partner; he is fully liable to third parties if his identity becomes known. An incoming partner is admitted after the firm's formation, while an outgoing partner retires or is expelled from the firm. 

**Conclusion:**
Thus, partners differ significantly in their participation, financial interest, and liability. While active and sleeping partners have full liability, nominal and holding-out partners become liable mainly because of their representation or association with the firm. A minor admitted to partnership benefits has no personal liability during minority.

**5. A partnership firm operates without a written Partnership Deed. During the year:**

**• Partners disagree on profit-sharing**
**• One partner claims interest on capital**
**• Another demands salary for managing the firm**

**Explain how these issues will be resolved as per the Indian Partnership Act, 1932.**

**Ans.**

**Resolution of Disputes in the Absence of a Partnership Deed**

A Partnership Deed is a written agreement containing the terms and conditions governing the mutual rights, duties, and liabilities of partners. Although a written deed is not compulsory under the Indian Partnership Act, 1932, a partnership can exist without one. When there is no written deed, or when the deed is silent on a particular matter, the provisions of the Act apply by default. 

**A) Disagreement Regarding Profit-Sharing:**
Where there is no Partnership Deed specifying the profit-sharing ratio, the profits and losses of the firm are shared equally among all partners. This rule applies irrespective of the amount of capital contributed by individual partners. Therefore, the partners' disagreement over profit-sharing will be resolved by distributing the profits equally among them. This provision is contained in Section 13(b) of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932. 

**B) Claim for Interest on Capital:**
The partner claiming interest on capital cannot receive such interest merely because capital has been contributed to the firm. In the absence of an agreement providing for interest on capital, no interest is payable on the partners' capital contributions. This is the default provision under Section 13(c) of the Act. Therefore, the partner's claim for interest on capital will not be allowed when there is no Partnership Deed or other agreement providing for it. 

**C) Demand for Salary for Managing the Firm:**
The partner who manages the firm's business is also not automatically entitled to salary. Under Section 13(a), no partner is entitled to salary, commission, or any other remuneration for taking part in the conduct of the business unless there is an agreement providing otherwise. Therefore, the partner's demand for salary will be rejected in the absence of an agreement allowing such remuneration. 

**D) Application of the Act:**
These provisions demonstrate that the Indian Partnership Act contains default rules for matters that have not been settled by agreement. The Act's provisions are residuary in nature and apply when the Partnership Deed is absent or silent. Partners are free to modify or exclude these provisions through an express agreement. 

**Conclusion:**
Thus, in the absence of a written Partnership Deed, profits and losses will be shared equally, no interest will be allowed on capital, and no partner will be entitled to salary for managing the firm. These statutory provisions provide uniform treatment and resolve the partners' claims unless a contrary agreement exists.

**1. Distinguish between the Profit and Loss Account and the Profit and Loss Appropriation Account of a partnership firm. Why are both accounts necessary?**

**Difference Between Profit and Loss Account and Profit and Loss Appropriation Account**

The Profit and Loss Account and Profit and Loss Appropriation Account are important parts of the final accounts of a partnership firm. They serve different purposes in determining and distributing the firm's profits.

| Basis | Profit and Loss Account | Profit and Loss Appropriation Account |
|---|---|---|
| **i) Purpose** | Ascertains the net profit or net loss of the firm. | Distributes the net profit among partners according to the Partnership Deed. |
| **ii) Nature** | Common to all forms of business. | Distinctive to partnership firms. |
| **iii) Starting Point** | Begins with gross profit or gross loss transferred from the Trading Account. | Begins with net profit or net loss transferred from the Profit and Loss Account. |
| **iv) Items Recorded** | Records indirect expenses, such as depreciation, bad debts, and administrative expenses, along with indirect incomes. | Records interest on capital, partners' salaries, commission, and interest on drawings. |
| **v) Treatment of Profit** | Determines the firm's net profit or net loss. | Determines the remaining distributable profit or loss after appropriations. |
| **vi) Final Transfer** | Transfers net profit or net loss to the Profit and Loss Appropriation Account. | Transfers the remaining profit or loss to partners' Capital or Current Accounts in the agreed profit-sharing ratio. |

**Why Are Both Accounts Necessary?**

**i) Determining Business Performance:** The Profit and Loss Account shows the firm's operating results by matching indirect income with indirect expenses.

**ii) Fair Distribution of Profit:** The Appropriation Account ensures that partners receive their entitled interest, salary, commission, and share of profit according to the Partnership Deed.

**Conclusion**

Both accounts are necessary because the Profit and Loss Account measures business performance, while the Appropriation Account ensures the correct distribution of profit among partners before preparing the Balance Sheet.

**2. What are post-closing adjustments? Describe, with an example, how such adjustments are recorded.**

**Ans.**

**Post-Closing Adjustments and Their Accounting Treatment**

Post-closing adjustments are correcting entries passed after the books of account for a year have been formally closed and profits have already been distributed among partners. They are required when errors or omissions are discovered after the preparation of final accounts. Since reopening the books and preparing the final accounts again is impractical, a rectifying journal entry is passed through the partners' Capital or Current Accounts.

**A) Situations Requiring Post-Closing Adjustments**

**i) Omission of Interest on Capital:** Interest on partners' capital may not have been credited or may have been calculated incorrectly.

**ii) Omission of Partner's Salary or Commission:** A partner's salary or commission may have been omitted or recorded at an incorrect amount.

**iii) Interest on Drawings:** Interest on drawings may not have been charged or may have been calculated incorrectly.

**iv) Incorrect Profit Distribution:** Profit may have been distributed among partners in the wrong ratio.

**B) Recording Post-Closing Adjustments**

The adjustment is recorded by calculating the actual profit distribution, determining the correct distribution, and finding the net effect on each partner. Partners who received excess profit are debited, while those who received less are credited. A single adjusting journal entry is passed through their Capital or Current Accounts.

**Example**

Suppose partners A and B share profits equally. A's salary of ₹24,000 was omitted, and profits had already been distributed.

A should have received ₹24,000 as salary, reducing the distributable profit by that amount. Therefore, B received ₹12,000 more profit than entitled, while A received ₹12,000 less.

**Rectifying Journal Entry:**

B's Capital A/c Dr. ₹12,000  
To A's Capital A/c ₹12,000

**Conclusion**

Post-closing adjustments correct errors in profit distribution without reopening the closed books or affecting the Profit and Loss Account.

**3. Define goodwill and list any four factors that determine the value of goodwill of a partnership firm.**

**Ans.**

**Goodwill and Factors Determining Its Value**

Goodwill is an intangible asset that represents the reputation, established clientele, business connections, and earning capacity of a firm beyond what is attributable to its tangible assets. It arises from the trust that customers, suppliers, and other stakeholders place in the firm. Goodwill enables a business to earn profits exceeding the normal rate of return for its industry. This excess profit is known as super profit.

In partnership accounting, goodwill becomes particularly important when a new partner is admitted, an existing partner retires or dies, or the profit-sharing ratio changes. It must be valued appropriately to compensate partners for changes in their share of the firm's earning capacity.

**A) Factors Determining the Value of Goodwill**

**i) Location:** A firm situated in a prominent commercial area or a location with high customer traffic generally enjoys higher goodwill than a firm in a remote area.

**ii) Nature of Business:** Businesses dealing in essential commodities or providing services with stable demand generally have higher goodwill. Businesses with volatile or seasonal demand may have lower goodwill.

**iii) Profitability and Track Record:** A firm with a consistent history of above-average profits enjoys greater goodwill. Unstable or declining profits reduce its value.

**iv) Quality of Management:** Competent, experienced, and reputable management enhances goodwill by ensuring efficient operations and sound business decisions.

**v) Market Standing and Brand:** An established brand, exclusive contracts, patents, or proprietary technology can increase goodwill.

**vi) Duration of Business:** Older firms with a proven business record generally enjoy higher goodwill than newly established enterprises.

**Conclusion**

Goodwill reflects a firm's reputation and ability to earn above-normal profits. Its value depends on factors such as location, profitability, management quality, and market standing.

**4. What is a Joint Life Policy? Explain the accounting treatment under the Surrender Value Method when the annual premium exceeds the increase in surrender value.**

**Ans.**

**Joint Life Policy and Accounting Treatment under the Surrender Value Method**

A Joint Life Policy (JLP) is a life insurance policy taken jointly on the lives of all partners of a firm. It provides financial security by ensuring that funds are available to settle the dues of a deceased partner without placing financial strain on the surviving partners or disrupting the business.

**A) Accounting Treatment under the Surrender Value Method**

Under this method, the Joint Life Policy is treated as an asset at its surrender value. The surrender value is the amount payable by the insurance company if the policy is surrendered before maturity.

**i) Payment of Annual Premium**

When the annual premium is paid, the increase in surrender value is debited to the Joint Life Policy Account, while the excess premium is charged to the Profit and Loss Account as an expense.

**Journal Entry:**

Profit and Loss A/c Dr. (Premium − Increase in Surrender Value)

Joint Life Policy A/c Dr. (Increase in Surrender Value)

To Bank A/c (Full Premium)

**ii) Example**

Suppose the annual premium is ₹40,000 and the surrender value increases by ₹25,000 during the year.

The amount charged to the Profit and Loss Account is:

₹40,000 − ₹25,000 = ₹15,000.

**Journal Entry:**

Profit and Loss A/c Dr. ₹15,000

Joint Life Policy A/c Dr. ₹25,000

To Bank A/c ₹40,000

**iii) Year-End Treatment**

The Joint Life Policy Account is maintained at the surrender value at the end of each year. Thus, the premium is divided between an asset component and an expense component.

**Conclusion**

Under the Surrender Value Method, the increase in surrender value is recorded as an asset, while the excess of the annual premium over that increase is charged to the Profit and Loss Account.

### Unit 2 Long Answer (400-500 words)

**1. Explain the complete process of preparing the final accounts of a partnership firm. How does the Balance Sheet presentation differ between the Fixed Capital Method and the Fluctuating Capital Method?**

**Ans.**

**Preparation of Final Accounts of a Partnership Firm and Difference in Capital Methods**

Final accounts are prepared at the end of the accounting year after ledger accounts have been balanced and the trial balance has been extracted and agreed. They summarise the firm's financial performance and position and ensure that profits or losses are distributed among partners according to the Partnership Deed.

**A) Process of Preparing Final Accounts**

**i) Trading Account**

The Trading Account is prepared first to ascertain gross profit or gross loss from buying and selling goods. Its debit side records opening stock, net purchases, and direct expenses such as wages, carriage inwards, and factory expenses. Its credit side records net sales and closing stock. The resulting gross profit or gross loss is transferred to the Profit and Loss Account.

**ii) Profit and Loss Account**

This account determines the firm's net profit or net loss. It begins with gross profit or gross loss from the Trading Account. Indirect expenses, such as administrative expenses, depreciation, bad debts, and interest on borrowings, are recorded on the debit side. Indirect incomes, such as commission received and rent received, are recorded on the credit side. The resulting net profit or loss is transferred to the Profit and Loss Appropriation Account.

**iii) Profit and Loss Appropriation Account**

This account distributes the firm's net profit among partners according to the Partnership Deed. It records interest on capital, partners' salaries and commission, where permitted by the deed, and interest on drawings. The remaining distributable profit or loss is transferred to the partners' Capital or Current Accounts in the agreed profit-sharing ratio.

**iv) Balance Sheet**

Finally, the Balance Sheet is prepared to show the firm's financial position at the end of the accounting year. It presents partners' capital balances, liabilities, and assets.

**B) Difference Between Fixed and Fluctuating Capital Methods**

| Basis | Fixed Capital Method | Fluctuating Capital Method |
|---|---|---|
| **i) Accounts Maintained** | Capital Account and Current Account for each partner. | Only one Capital Account for each partner. |
| **ii) Capital Balance** | Remains constant unless capital is formally changed. | Changes every year according to transactions. |
| **iii) Adjustments** | Recorded in the Current Account. | Recorded directly in the Capital Account. |
| **iv) Balance Sheet Presentation** | Shows fixed Capital Account and separate Current Account balances for each partner. | Shows only one Capital Account balance for each partner, including all adjustments. |

Under the Fixed Capital Method, a debit balance in a partner's Current Account appears on the assets side as a current asset, while a credit balance appears on the liabilities side. Under the Fluctuating Capital Method, adjustments are incorporated into the single Capital Account balance.

**Conclusion**

The four final accounts establish gross profit, net profit, profit distribution, and financial position. The capital method determines how partners' balances are maintained and presented in the Balance Sheet.

**2. What is goodwill? Describe in detail the Average Profits Method, Super Profits Method, and Capitalisation Method of valuing goodwill. Highlight the key differences among these methods.**

**Ans.**

**Goodwill and Methods of Valuation**

Goodwill is an intangible asset representing a firm's reputation, established clientele, brand value, and capacity to earn profits above the normal rate of return. It becomes important in partnership accounting when the firm's constitution changes, such as during admission, retirement, or a change in the profit-sharing ratio.

**A) Average Profits Method**

Under this method, goodwill is calculated by multiplying the average adjusted profits earned over a representative period, usually three to five years, by the agreed number of years' purchase.

**Formula:**

Goodwill = Average Profits × Number of Years' Purchase

Average Profits = Total Adjusted Past Profits ÷ Number of Years

Before calculating average profits, abnormal losses are added back, abnormal gains are deducted, and other necessary adjustments are made. This method values goodwill based on the firm's historical earning capacity.

**B) Super Profits Method**

Super profit is the excess of average actual profits over normal profit. Normal profit is the amount a similar firm with comparable capital and risk would be expected to earn.

**Formulas:**

Normal Profit = Capital Employed × Normal Rate of Return ÷ 100

Super Profit = Average Actual Profit − Normal Profit

Goodwill = Super Profit × Number of Years' Purchase

This method recognises goodwill only when the firm earns more than the normal profit. If average actual profit does not exceed normal profit, goodwill is nil.

**C) Capitalisation Method**

This method calculates the capitalised value of the firm's earnings and deducts actual capital employed to determine goodwill. It can be applied using average profits or super profits.

**Capitalisation of Average Profits:**

Capitalised Value = Average Profits × (100 ÷ Normal Rate of Return)

Goodwill = Capitalised Value − Actual Capital Employed

**Capitalisation of Super Profits:**

Goodwill = Super Profit × (100 ÷ Normal Rate of Return)

**D) Key Differences Among the Methods**

| Basis | Average Profits Method | Super Profits Method | Capitalisation Method |
|---|---|---|---|
| **i) Basis** | Average adjusted profits | Excess over normal profit | Capitalised earnings |
| **ii) Calculation** | Average profit × years' purchase | Super profit × years' purchase | Capitalised value less actual capital, or capitalised super profit |
| **iii) Normal Profit** | Not deducted | Deducted to calculate super profit | Normal rate of return is used |
| **iv) Main Focus** | Historical profitability | Above-normal earning capacity | Value of earnings relative to capital employed |

**Conclusion**

All three methods estimate goodwill differently. The Average Profits Method considers historical profits, the Super Profits Method focuses on excess earnings, and the Capitalisation Method determines goodwill through capitalised earnings.

**3. What accounting adjustments are required when existing partners decide to change their profit-sharing ratio? Explain the concepts of sacrificing ratio, gaining ratio, goodwill adjustment, and revaluation of assets and liabilities with suitable examples.**

**Ans.**

**Accounting Adjustments on Change in Profit-Sharing Ratio**

When existing partners decide to change their profit-sharing ratio, some partners gain a larger share of future profits while others sacrifice a portion of their share. Accounting adjustments are necessary to ensure that goodwill and revaluation gains or losses are distributed fairly before the new ratio takes effect.

**i) Sacrificing Ratio and Gaining Ratio**

A sacrificing partner gives up a portion of their profit share, whereas a gaining partner receives an additional share.

**Formulas:**

Sacrificing Ratio = Old Ratio − New Ratio

Gaining Ratio = New Ratio − Old Ratio

For example, A and B share profits equally in the old ratio of 1:1. They change their ratio to 3:1. A's old share is 1/2 and new share is 3/4, so A gains 1/4. B's old share is 1/2 and new share is 1/4, so B sacrifices 1/4. Thus, A is the gaining partner and B is the sacrificing partner.

**ii) Goodwill Adjustment**

Goodwill represents the firm's reputation, established clientele, and capacity to earn super profits. When the profit-sharing ratio changes, gaining partners benefit from the firm's goodwill and must compensate sacrificing partners.

When goodwill is not to appear in the books, the gaining partners' Capital or Current Accounts are debited, while the sacrificing partners' accounts are credited.

For example, if goodwill is valued at ₹80,000 and A gains 1/4 while B sacrifices 1/4, A must compensate B by ₹20,000 (₹80,000 × 1/4).

Journal Entry:

A's Capital A/c Dr. ₹20,000  
To B's Capital A/c ₹20,000

**iii) Revaluation of Assets and Liabilities**

Assets and liabilities are revalued at their current fair values when the profit-sharing ratio changes. This ensures that unrealised gains or losses arising under the old ratio are distributed among the partners according to that ratio.

A Revaluation Account, also called the Profit and Loss Adjustment Account, is prepared for this purpose.

- Decreases in asset values and increases in liability values are debited to the Revaluation Account.
- Increases in asset values and decreases in liability values are credited to the Revaluation Account.
- The resulting profit or loss is transferred to all partners' Capital or Current Accounts in the old ratio.

For example, if land increases in value by ₹50,000 and the partners share profits equally, the revaluation profit of ₹50,000 is distributed equally, giving each partner ₹25,000.

**Conclusion**

Changing the profit-sharing ratio requires calculating sacrificing and gaining ratios, adjusting goodwill, and revaluing assets and liabilities. These adjustments ensure that gains and losses belonging to the old arrangement are settled fairly before the new ratio takes effect.

**4. Discuss the Joint Life Policy as a financial planning tool for a partnership firm. Explain the full accounting treatment —from annual premium payment through to distribution of policy proceeds—under both the Surrender Value Method and the JLP Fund Method.**

**Ans.**

**Joint Life Policy and Its Accounting Treatment**

A Joint Life Policy (JLP) is an insurance policy taken jointly on the lives of all partners in a partnership firm. It provides financial security to the firm by ensuring that funds are available upon the death of a partner. These funds help the firm settle the deceased partner's dues without creating financial difficulties for the continuing partners.

**i) Surrender Value Method**

Under this method, the Joint Life Policy is treated as an asset at its surrender value. The annual premium paid is divided into two parts: the increase in surrender value, which is recorded as an asset, and the remaining amount, which is charged to the Profit and Loss Account as an expense.

For example, if the annual premium is ₹40,000 and the surrender value increases by ₹25,000, the accounting treatment is:

Profit and Loss A/c Dr. ₹15,000  
Joint Life Policy A/c Dr. ₹25,000  
To Bank A/c ₹40,000

The Joint Life Policy Account is maintained at the policy's surrender value at the end of each year.

When a partner dies or the policy matures, the insurance proceeds become receivable. The Insurance Company Account is debited with the policy amount, while the Joint Life Policy Account is credited with its book value. The balancing amount represents profit on the policy.

The profit is distributed among all partners, including the deceased partner, in their old profit-sharing ratio.

**ii) Joint Life Policy Fund Method**

Under this method, the annual premium is treated as an expense, and an equal amount is credited to the Joint Life Policy Fund. The fund accumulates over the years to provide financial protection to the firm.

The accounting entries for annual premium payment are:

Profit and Loss A/c Dr.  
To Bank A/c

Joint Life Policy Reserve or Fund A/c Dr.  
To Joint Life Policy Fund A/c

The premium is charged to the Profit and Loss Account, while the corresponding amount is transferred to the Joint Life Policy Fund. The fund is accumulated until the policy proceeds become payable.

Upon the death of a partner or maturity of the policy, the accumulated fund is distributed among the partners in their old profit-sharing ratio. The insurance proceeds are received from the insurance company, and the deceased partner's dues are settled using the available funds.

**iii) Difference Between the Two Methods**

Under the Surrender Value Method, the policy is recorded as an asset at its surrender value, and only the excess of the premium over the increase in surrender value is charged to the Profit and Loss Account. Under the JLP Fund Method, the entire premium is treated as an expense, and an equal amount is credited to the JLP Fund.

**Conclusion**

Both methods help a partnership firm plan financially for a partner's death. The Surrender Value Method recognises the policy's surrender value as an asset, whereas the JLP Fund Method accumulates a separate fund to provide financial security and help settle the deceased partner's dues.

**Rectification of Errors Through Post-Closing Adjustments**

Post-closing adjustments are made when omissions or errors in the distribution of profit are discovered after the partnership books have been formally closed. Instead of reopening the books, a single rectifying journal entry is passed through the partners' Capital or Current Accounts.

**i) Calculation of Net Adjustment**

The old profit-sharing ratio of Nidhi, Omkar, and Payal is 3:2:1.

Interest on Capital:
Nidhi = ₹18,000
Omkar = ₹12,000
Payal = ₹8,000
Total = ₹38,000

Nidhi's Salary = ₹36,000

Interest on Drawings:
Nidhi = ₹5,400
Omkar = ₹3,600
Payal = ₹1,800
Total = ₹10,800

Net reduction in distributable profit = ₹38,000 + ₹36,000 − ₹10,800 = ₹63,200.

This excess profit was distributed in the old ratio of 3:2:1.

Nidhi's excess profit = ₹63,200 × 3/6 = ₹31,600
Omkar's excess profit = ₹63,200 × 2/6 = ₹21,066.67
Payal's excess profit = ₹63,200 × 1/6 = ₹10,533.33

(Being rectification of omitted interest on capital, salary, and interest on drawings for the year ended 31st March 2024.)

**Conclusion**

The rectifying entry transfers the net adjustment from the partners who received excess profit to the partner entitled to additional credit. It corrects the distribution without reopening the closed books or affecting the Profit and Loss Account.

**2. Compute goodwill of the firm of Rajan & Suman using all three methods from the following data:**

**Past profits: Year 1 ₹2,00,000; Year 2 ₹2,50,000; Year 3 ₹1,80,000 (after charging abnormal loss of ₹40,000); Year 4 ₹3,00,000; Year 5 ₹2,80,000 (including abnormal gain ₹50,000).**
**Capital employed: ₹15,00,000. Normal rate of return: 12%.**
**Years' purchase for Average Profits Method: 3; for Super Profits Method: 5.**

**Ans.**

**Calculation of Goodwill of Rajan & Suman**

**i) Calculation of Adjusted Profits**

Abnormal losses are added back, while abnormal gains are deducted to calculate adjusted profits.

Average Adjusted Profit = ₹12,00,000 ÷ 5 = ₹2,40,000.

**ii) Average Profits Method**

Goodwill = Average Profits × Years' Purchase

= ₹2,40,000 × 3

**iii) Super Profits Method**

= ₹1,80,000

= ₹60,000

Goodwill = Super Profit × Years' Purchase

= ₹60,000 × 5

Under the Capitalisation Method, goodwill is calculated by capitalising average profits at the normal rate of return and deducting actual capital employed.

Capitalised Value = Average Profits × (100 ÷ Normal Rate of Return)

= ₹2,40,000 × (100 ÷ 12)
